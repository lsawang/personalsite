Triceratops

Building a website for a client we know and love.

Our team of 6 designed and developed the website for a UC Davis club that loves dinos, where we turned a Discord and Instagram-ran operation into something a first-year can find & understand on their first visit.

Overview

Triceratops is a UC Davis club that crochets dinosaurs and hides them around campus to be found and collected.

Our client relies on Instagram for reach and Discord for everything else. This includes drop announcements, rules, flash sales, and trading. Previously, there was no website, so anyone outside the Discord had no way in.

I work with my team of six, my role consists of not only the design, research, and aesthetics of the site, but also the development and creation of it all.

This project is still an ongoing project with the club, as we are still iterating and developing the site.

Problem

Nobody could explain how to participate, including people who already had

Rules live in multiple, scattered Discord channels. The post-claim process is nowhere, and the Instagram-to-Discord handoff is invisible.

Newcomers also arrived with three common misconceptions of the club:

  • You need to know how to crochet.
  • Meetings are mandatory.
  • It costs money to join.

Why this matters

Access

Word-of-mouth is the main source of information for new members, so anyone without friends on the inside remains outside.

Retention

The members need ways of involvement that go beyond drops, but such opportunities are hidden in Discord servers.

Guiding question

How might we make joining a dino hunt obvious to someone who has never heard of the club?

Research

Understanding the club from three perspectives

We ran interviews and three survey cohorts, sorting every participant by how close or far they already were to the club. The three different views of the same set of questions served to differentiate onboarding issues from member-related issues.

Ideation

Skim first, depth underneath

Someone should be able to skim what the club is and know how a drop works initially, then go deeper if they want. We mapped the site into four areas and drew flows for the two journeys that mattered: joining for the first time, and claiming a dino you just found.

Design System

Because we build it too, the system had to be handoff-proof

Participants described the aesthetic before we proposed one: colorful, whimsical, "like a crayon box." We established the typography scales for both desktop and mobile platforms, a UI color palette complete with contrast ratios, radii, grids, and states for buttons.

Design Exploration

I designed too much, had to remind myself that simpler is better

My first hi-fi design leaned on animations mostly: hover states on every card, pop-ups, and reveal animations. Put together, they made the page busy and caused too much friction. This was also said in the usability tests. This did not appeal to the club’s vision they wanted. Simpler held up better.

✗ Rejected

Animation on everything

Hover effects, pop-ups, and animated reveals across the page. Fun in isolation, but stacked together they lowered the premium feel and pulled attention away from the content people came for.

✓ Chosen

Animations where it made sense

Kept the whimsy in illustration, color, and copy, and reserved motion for a few deliberate moments. There is such a thing as too much, and restraint is what made the site feel finished.

Testing

Testers found the Rules page. They could not find the part they needed.

The initial page I designed was intuitive for almost everyone. The labels and sections were good but the hierarchy wasn’t. The page itself was too lengthy causing scrolling fatigue and the post-claim steps sat at the bottom. Some testers also stated that they thought the steps would’ve been in the Getting Started page. Testers also noted that the visual background wasn’t ideal and were not a fan of the gradient.

What changed between V1 and V2

V1

To solve the scrolling fatigue, I hid each rule behind a rectangle that opened a pop-up card on click. It kept the page short.

FB

My lead pointed out that the extra click caused friction. The rules needed to be visible initially and directly.

V2

I surfaced the rules on the page itself. V1 had also drifted off-theme while we were still exploring directions, so V2 follows our set design system.

Insights

Receiving feedback from not only user-testing, but from a lead and the client itself was such an interesting yet positive experience. Perspectives from all 3 point-of-views led to learning new things every time, hearing feedback and suggestions that I would’ve never noticed myself, and growing and changing little by little as a designer. I value every opportunity to improve as a designer and I also learned so much more about all the platforms I used during this project.

Final Solution

A public site that answers questions so the Discord doesn’t need to anymore.
