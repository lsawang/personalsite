Overview

CycleFind helps people find menstrual products nearby, without making people feel insecure in their search.

CycleFind is a mobile app designed to help people locate free and low-cost menstrual products in their area. This app is designed for users who can browse anonymously, check whether products are currently available, track their cycle, and access menstrual health information all in a single app.

My Role

I worked independently on this project from the design process and research, to the ideation and finding problems/solutions, as well as creating user flows, wireframes, hi-fis, user-testing, and prototypes.

The Problem

Period products are basic necessities. Access to them isn't always basic.

Period poverty affects people who can't consistently afford menstrual products, but the problem isn't limited to cost. Even when free products are available through campuses, shelters, health centers, and community organizations, people still have to figure out where they are, whether they’re actually in stock, and how to access them. “In the United States, 40% (or 2 in 5) of adults and 25% (or 1 in 4) of students/teenagers struggle to afford or access period products.” period.org/periodpoverty

My research pointed to another issue that was harder to design around: stigma. Someone looking for a pad shouldn't have to announce that they need financial help, create an account, or arrive somewhere only to discover that the products are gone or not there.

Guiding question

How might I make finding menstrual resources feel as private, reliable, and ordinary as looking up any other place on a map?

Research

The resource isn't useful if people don't trust it.

I started by researching who experiences period poverty, what prevents people from accessing existing resources, and what CycleFind would need to do differently from a normal Google search.

My primary audience became younger users roughly 14-26, mostly high school and college students with limited income, this includes people with limited mobile data and/or housing insecurity. Four things kept coming up.

01

Privacy matters

Stigma can stop someone from looking for help even when resources exist, so anonymous browsing became a very important core feature rather than a setting. No profile needed and no interactions with others.

02

Availability has to feel trustworthy

A nearby resource doesn’t mean much if the products are gone, so listings had to show if products were stocked, low, or unavailable.

03

The experience needs to be quick

Users may already be stressed especially if they’re in an emergency situation. They shouldn't have to navigate a complicated interface or go through unnecessary friction to find a pad or tampon.

04

A resource finder alone isn't enough

If CycleFind only solved one emergency, there was little reason to open it again. Cycle tracking and Learn create ongoing value.

The principle that came out of it

Make asking for help feel like finding information.

Participants consistently described the app as straightforward, and the issues were small moments of friction rather than navigation failures. In the second round the Log Today action was easier to find, onboarding felt more natural, spacing felt less cramped, and the language was clearer. They also responded well to the new Learn tab, especially the way articles connected back to their current cycle phase. All the features were praised and most said they would actually use the app.

The Outcome

The final prototype brought together three needs that initially felt separate: access, planning, and education.

Finding free and affordable menstrual products turned out to be something which also could serve as preparation for the user’s next cycle and provide some insight into the user’s menstrual condition. More importantly, the usability tests demonstrated that the new features didn’t make the interface feel heavier. Users kept saying that the interface was still clear and coherent.
