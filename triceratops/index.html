Case Study 01

Simplifying the rules flow so students can start hunting faster

Designing the first front door for a club that lived in a group chat.

Triceratops is a UC Davis club that crochets dinosaurs and hides them across campus for students to find and claim, while club members help make the hunts happen. It ran entirely on Discord and Instagram, so anyone outside the server had no way in, and the club wanted a site where anyone can access information so no one is left confused. My team of 6 designed and built its first public website.

Problem:

Finding information about Triceratops was complex.

Rules live in multiple, scattered Discord channels. The post-claim process is nowhere, and the Instagram-to-Discord handoff is invisible.

Newcomers also arrived with three common misconceptions: you need to know how to crochet, meetings are mandatory, and it costs money to join.

My goal and challenge was to condense the rules spread across an entire Discord server into one page a newcomer could scan in under a minute.

Design decisions:

Skim first, depth underneath

Taking a step back

My first hi-fi design leaned heavily on animation: hover states on every card, pop-ups, and reveal animations. Put together, they made the page busy and competed with the content. Testers said the pop-up overlays and overuse of animation caused too much user friction. I cut it back to hover states only so it gave the page a more “premium-feel.”

✓ Chosen

Animations where it made sense

After setting the design system, I matched the design to the identity, removed the hover blur, replaced pop-ups with scannable boxes, and used icons and visual weight (bolding) so the rules read easily. I kept the whimsy in illustration, color, and copy, and limited motion to hover states on things you can click. There is such a thing as too much, and restraint is what made the site feel finished.

✗ Rejected

Animation on everything

Hover effects, pop-ups, and animated reveals across the page. Fun in isolation, but stacked together they lowered the premium feel and pulled attention away from the content people came for. The glass-effect gradient didn’t match the surrounding theme, and the rules still carried too much cognitive load.

Testing:

Testers found the Rules page. They could not find the part they needed.

I ran my first round of usability testing after the mid-fidelity layout. Testers reached the Rules page, but the post-claim steps were buried at the bottom, and that feedback set the tone for my hi-fi.
