The Problem

College musicians have music, but not the place where to put it.

During our initial interviews, the majority said almost the same thing: when they need some review of their tracks, they send those tracks to friends or post on Discord and wait for someone to answer.

However, there is an issue with this system: They consist of file size limits, unclear reviews/reaction (“it is good”), and inability to assess whether the reviewer is credible or not.

Guiding question

How might we build a platform where musicians can share unfinished work, get feedback they can actually use, and find people to make music with?

Research

Four patterns kept surfacing in the affinity map.

I interviewed and surveyed student musicians across instruments and genres: producers, vocalists, multi-instrumentalists, people who’d never been in a band but wanted to be.

Where the gap is

We mapped Harmonize against Vampr and BandLab to see what already existed.

{{ c.text }}

Vampr is a paid swipe-and-match networking app for business professionals. BandLab is a free, full production suite for content creators who already have ideas of what they want to create. Neither of them actually had any sort of feedback culture ingrained within them. This is the area where Harmonize exists, a free platform where musicians can get their sound critiqued and reviewed.

Typography

Inter

Display / UI / body

Inter is used for clean UI and is legible in both large headers and smaller sizes while it does not conflict with any visuals or displays.

In order to give Harmonize a more welcoming feel compared to the average productivity-blue networking app, we decided on using plum as our dark color palette, terracotta as well as warm sand instead of one being vibrant while the other is subdued and finally eucalyptus as the only cool color, which is used sparingly in toggles and success states.
